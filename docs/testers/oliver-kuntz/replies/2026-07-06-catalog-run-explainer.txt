THE METHOD, PLAINLY

The engine hunts for pairs of technologies whose descriptions share
almost no vocabulary but describe structurally similar mechanisms or
complementary unmet needs. Concretely, every description becomes two
signatures: a lexical one (what words it uses) and a semantic one
(what it means). The interesting pairs are the ones where those two
signatures disagree: meaning-close, vocabulary-far. That differential
is the raw material of the idea Lawrence pitched you: take 500 rows,
uncover the gem nobody noticed, combine three low-scorers into one
high.

One discipline matters: a high differential is necessary, not
sufficient. The top of a raw list can be paraphrases wearing
different vocabulary. A second component, the Grounding Guard, is
being built now to filter restatements and confident noise BEFORE
anything reaches your desk. Nothing gets called an opportunity until
it passes that filter.

WHAT BROKE, AND WHERE IT STANDS

1. Memory. The embedding step tried to process all 2,128
   descriptions in a single pass and asked the machine for roughly
   27GB of RAM. Root cause: no batching loop. Fix: process in
   batches of 32. Status: landed today.

2. The offline fallback path (the no-model mode) fails to load its
   vector extension in this environment. Tracked as the next
   infrastructure item in the queue.

A FIRST LOOK, ALREADY VISIBLE

Even before the scoring engine runs, we mapped the catalog's own
cross-domain pair list into a relationship graph: 386 technologies,
1,038 problem-sharing links across 84 shared problems (biggest:
neurodegeneration, respiratory disease, inflammation, autoimmunity).
One technology sits at the center with 44 separate cross-domain
links, more than any other in the catalog. That is the shape of the
gem-hunting Lawrence described: the most combinable asset you own,
surfaced by structure, not by memory. It is a map of leads, not
verdicts, and none of it has passed the Grounding Guard yet. But it
shows the payoff is real and already in your data.
