# Feedback: study-app-001

Your pitch delivers every structural element the assignment asked for, and delivers each one concretely. The gap is not what is missing -- it is what is unproven. The two claims your entire venture rests on (that time scarcity is the real problem, and that summaries plus quizzes fix it) are the only two you did not back with anything. You already sensed this when you named market research and user testing as your own gaps.

## The plan is complete and concrete -- this is the part that works. The criterion is specifics, not gestures: a prototype, risks with mitigations, a critical path, a team. You went four for four. You did not settle for a good team -- you named three roles and said why each is essential. That is the standard.

- The prototype would include a simple mobile interface with three main features: summaries, flashcards, and short quizzes after each topic.
- One risk is low user engagement, which I would solve by adding gamification like points and progressive tracking.
- Another risk is inaccurate content, which would be handled by vali- validating materials with experts or trusted sources.
- The critical path is, uh, idea, content, development, testing, and launch. And the project would take around ten to twelve weeks.
- The initial team would include a software developer, a UX/UI designer, and a content creator. Each one is essential for building, designing, and producing study material.

Next step: Put the plan on a calendar. Take the four tasks you named -- data collection, content creation, app development, and testing -- and assign each a week inside your ten-to-twelve-week window. Then mark the earliest week you could put the app in front of a real student. That date is your first testable milestone, and naming it turns a list into a schedule.

## Your two load-bearing claims are asserted, not evidenced. An evidenced claim points at something you observed or read; an asserted claim just says it is so. Nobody expects a study of your own at this stage -- but they do expect you to know which of your claims are still bets. The problem statement and the efficacy promise are the two the venture rests on, and they are the only two with nothing behind them.

- Students often struggle with large amounts of study material and limited time.
- My app solves this by breaking content into short summaries, flashcards, and quizzes that improve understanding and memory.
- Nothing in the pitch tells us how you know either one. No survey, no interview, not one observed student -- and no source behind the efficacy promise. It sounds obviously true, and that is exactly the danger: obviously-true problems are the ones nobody checks. There is real published work on retrieval practice and spaced repetition that would back you here; one cited line moves this from a bet to a claim.

Next step: Do not build anything yet. Find five students in the library during exam period and ask what they actually did in their last two study hours -- not what they wish they had done. You are testing one thing: whether the binding constraint is really time, or whether it is not knowing what to study. If four of five say the second, your problem statement changes and your app changes with it. Five interviews is one week of work, and it is the highest-leverage week you have.

## You named your own gaps, unprompted. That is a real capability and most of this class does not demonstrate it. It is scored as credit above -- not re-listed back to you as a deficiency someone else discovered. The next move is not to name them again. It is to know what closing one actually looks like.

- You closed by naming deeper market research, competitor analysis, and user testing as gaps in your own work, with nobody asking. Self-assessment at this level is rarer than a working prototype, and it is why this pitch scores where it does on the final question.

Next step: Close competitor analysis first -- it is the cheapest and you can finish it this week. Download Quizlet, Anki, and one AI study app. Spend twenty minutes inside each and write one page: what each already does with summaries, flashcards, and quizzes, and the single thing yours would do that they do not. If you cannot fill that last line, that is the most valuable finding you will get all semester -- far better to find it now than in week ten of your build.
