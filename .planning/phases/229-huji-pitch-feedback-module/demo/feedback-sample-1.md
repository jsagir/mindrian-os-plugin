# Feedback: safescan-001

You named the problem precisely -- diners cannot trust an assurance nobody can verify -- and then built a pitch that asks for exactly that. The skeleton is all there: problem, value, mechanism, risks, roadmap, team gaps. What is missing under every one of them is a reason to believe. Your device's whole value is replacing trust with evidence, so the claim that it detects allergens is the one claim you cannot simply assert.

## The architecture is sound, and that is the hard part: your value proposition answers the exact problem you named, in the exact moment it bites. The problem is a scene -- a plate, a waiter, a decision -- and the solution lands in that same scene, at the table, under a minute. Most first pitches solve a category; you solved a moment. The three steps then make it feel real without a word of jargon.

- Restaurant kitchen cannot guarantee one hundred percent safe food, so diners are often afraid to trust the waiter.
- It is a tiny device that tests your food right at the table and gives you a scientific answer in less than a minute.
- Using it is very simple. Just three quick steps.

Next step: Read your problem line and your value line back to back to five classmates, then ask them what the device does and when they would use it. If all five answer both, the architecture holds -- spend your entire next revision on evidence instead.

## Every claim in this pitch is asserted, and the one carrying all the weight cannot be. A smart light sensor is a category of thing, not a result; tiny traces is not a quantity; high-quality testing materials is a value, not a mitigation. Notice the recursion: your own problem statement says people are afraid to trust an assurance with nothing behind it -- and right now, SafeScan is that assurance.

- The science inside uses smart light sensor. This technology detects even tiny traces of allergen proteins that the human eye cannot see.
- Food allergies are a big problem around the world.
- We care about accuracy and affordability. We use high-quality testing materials to prevent mistakes

Next step: Pick the sensor claim and give it one number. Find one published paper on optical allergen detection, cite the detection limit it reports, and say in one line whether your device needs to beat it. One sourced number under your central claim moves this pitch further than any new slide.

## You named your own capability gaps and split them into now and later, each tied to a reason -- real self-assessment, where most first pitches claim a complete team. So the work is not finding more gaps; it is aiming them. The risk that should drive your order is the one you never named: a false negative -- your device says safe, and the food is not. Yet the safety expert arrives later, certification rides along with launch, and user beta testing sits at step two, ahead of both.

- our initial recruitment plan is to hire a hardware and biosensor engineer for the device
- Later, as we grow, we'll bring in a safety expert for official health certifications
- Next, we'll do user beta testing and then achieve market launch and certification.

Next step: Finish this sentence in writing: If SafeScan says safe and the food is not, then ___. Then re-order your roadmap so the safety expert lands before you put the device in front of users, not after growth. That sentence is your riskiest assumption, and the assignment wants you to find it before the market does.
